How to choose a claw clips

Updated June 2026Independently researchedNo paid placement.

Quick answer

To choose the right claw clip, focus on three things: size (match your hair length and thickness), material (acetate for grip, plastic for lightweight hold, metal springs for extra tension), and tooth design (wide-set for thick hair, fine for thin). Look for smooth edges and a spring that feels sturdy but not painfully tight, and don ’t chase a single “ best ” brand, the best clip is the one that holds your hair all day without slipping or snagging.

What size claw clip should I get?

Claw clips come in small, medium, large, and extra‑large, but those labels are not standardized across brands. For short hair (above the shoulders) or fine hair that is not very dense, a small or medium clip is usually plenty, anything bigger can look overwhelming and may not grip enough hair to stay put. If you have shoulder‑length hair that is medium thickness, a large clip often works well. For very long or very thick hair, look for an extra‑large clip (sometimes called a “ jumbo ” or “ maxi ” claw). Owners of thick or curly hair routinely say even a large clip can feel undersized. A good rule of thumb: the clip should be able to close around your gathered hair with the teeth still overlapping by at least half a centimetre, if it barely snaps shut, you need a bigger size. Many brands offer multi‑packs that include several sizes, which is a smart way to experiment without committing to a single size. Keep in mind that the shape of the clip matters too, a wider, flatter clip distributes pressure differently than a tall, rounded one.

Which material is best for claw clips?

The two most common materials are acetate (a plant‑based plastic) and standard injection‑molded plastic (often polystyrene or similar). Acetate clips are generally sturdier, have a warm, glossy look, and feel smoother to the touch. They also tend to hold tension longer because the material has a bit of flexibility without becoming brittle. Many people with fine hair prefer acetate because the teeth are often less sharp. All‑plastic clips are lighter and usually cheaper, but the hinge can wear out faster and the teeth may have moulding seams that snag hair. A few premium brands use metal springs inside plastic or acetate bodies, which gives a much stronger, more reliable hold. That design is especially helpful if your hair is heavy or slick. Avoid clips with visible rough edges or flash (excess plastic) around the teeth. Even a tiny burr can cause breakage over time. If you like to swim or shower with a clip, look for one labeled as “ acetate ” with an open, easy‑to‑rinse design, all‑plastic hinges can trap water and get sticky.

How can I get a secure hold without pulling or breaking hair?

A secure hold comes from three things: the spring tension, the tooth shape, and how you position the clip. Springs that feel “ medium ” tension are generally best, too tight and the clip will pull hairs when you take it out; too loose and it will slide. Published owner feedback often mentions that high‑end brands (like France Luxe or Kitsch) have a more consistent spring tension than no‑name fast‑fashion clips. Tooth design: wide, rounded teeth are gentler on thick or curly hair and let the clip sit more loosely without crushing volume. Fine, closely spaced teeth are better for thin hair because they grab individual strands more effectively. If you have slick or fine hair, look for clips with interior ridges or a slight curve that cups the scalp. When putting the clip in, twist your hair into a loose bun or fold, then slide the clip in at a 45‑degree angle instead of straight down. This lets the teeth catch more layers. For very long hair, clip it upside down (teeth pointing toward your scalp) and then flip the clip upright, a trick many long‑haired reviewers swear by for all‑day hold.

How do I choose a claw clip for my hair type?

For fine or thin hair: a medium clip with fine, closely spaced teeth works best. Avoid oversized clips that may not be able to grip a small amount of hair, and look for a lightweight plastic or thin‑acetate body so the clip doesn ’t weigh your hair down. A “ mini ” or “ small ” claw is often the most practical choice. For thick or coarse hair: you need a large or extra‑large clip with wide, smooth teeth. The clip should have a strong spring but not a sharp bite. Acetate or metal‑spring designs hold up better over time. Some clips are specifically marketed as “ thick‑hair ” or “ voluminous‑hair ” clips, owners confirm they do tend to have a longer jaw opening. For curly or textured hair: look for claw clips with a wide, open shape that won ’t flatten your curls. A “ banana‑style ” clip (longer and narrower) often works well for curly hair because it holds the twist without crushing volume. Smooth edges are critical, curly hair is more prone to snagging. Some brands add a silicone grip inside the clip, which is helpful for keeping slippery, conditioned curls in place.

What style of claw clip works for everyday wear versus special occasions?

For daily wear, a simple, low‑profile clip in a neutral color (tortoiseshell, black, clear) is the most versatile. Look for a clip that lies flat against the head so it is comfortable to lean back against a chair or car headrest. Rounded, unadorned designs are less likely to catch on scarves or collars. For formal or dressy occasions, claw clips are now made with decorative elements: gold or silver plating, embedded resin flowers, pearl accents, or carved detail. These still function as a hair holder but add an intentional accessory look. The trade‑off is that decorated clips are often heavier and may not hold as securely, so it is wise to test the grip before wearing them for a long event. There is also a trend toward “ french ” or “ minimal ” claw clips that are smaller and sit like a decorative barrette. These are best for half‑up styles or for holding back bangs rather than securing a full bun. If you want one clip that does both casual and polished, a medium‑large tortoiseshell acetate clip in a classic shape is the most universally recommended choice.

Frequently asked questions

Do claw clips damage hair over time?

They can if the clip has rough edges, a too‑tight spring, or if you constantly put it in the exact same spot. To minimize damage, choose clips with smooth, polished teeth and avoid pulling the clip out sideways, always open it fully and lift straight up. Never sleep in a claw clip, as friction against a pillow can cause breakage.

How should I store claw clips to make them last?

Keep them in a dry place away from direct sunlight, because UV light can make acetate brittle and fade colours. Avoid tossing them loose in a bag where they can get scratched; a small pouch or a dedicated section in a drawer works well. If a clip loses tension, you can sometimes gently bend the metal spring back with pliers, but for acetate clip‑on hinges, replacement is safer.

Can I use a claw clip on wet hair?

Yes, but be gentle. Wet hair is more elastic and prone to breakage. Use a clip with wide, smooth teeth and never yank it out while the hair is wet. If you clip a wet bun while sleeping, you risk creating crimps and tangles. Many people find a large, open claw clip useful for holding a twisted bun while letting the hair air‑dry.

How do I keep a claw clip from slipping out of my hair?

First, check that the clip size matches your hair mass, an oversized clip on fine hair will slip. You can also try a “ twist and tuck ” method: twist your hair, fold the twist upward, and clamp the clip across the fold. For extra grip, look for clips with interior silicone strips or ridges. Some people lightly spray their hair with dry shampoo or texturizing spray before clipping, which adds friction.

Are claw clips suitable for short hair?

Absolutely. Mini and small claw clips are perfect for short hair, bob cuts, or pixie styles. They work well to hold back the front sections or to secure a small top knot. Avoid large clips, they will not be able to grab enough hair to stay put. Some brands offer “ baby ” claws that are barely two inches long, ideal for fine, short layers.

What is the difference between a claw clip and a jaw clip?

The terms are often used interchangeably, but generally a “ jaw clip ” has a more rounded, single‑piece body with inner teeth that snap together, while a “ claw clip ” can have two separate arms that hinge open wider. Jaw clips tend to hold more hair and distribute pressure evenly, but they can be bulkier. Which one you prefer is largely personal, try both shapes to see which feels more secure on your hair.

In shortA great claw clip is a simple tool that makes a big difference in your daily routine if you choose thoughtfully. Pay attention to how the clip feels in your hand, whether the hinge snaps with confidence, and whether the teeth are kind to your hair. A few dollars more for a well‑made clip is almost always worth it, because a clip that fits will stay put, last years, and never give you a headache. Start with one or two versatile sizes in a classic material, then branch out once you know what your hair really needs.
